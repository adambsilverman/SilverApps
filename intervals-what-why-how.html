Intervals: What, Why and How

An interval is the distance between two notes. Step through the lesson, play with each picture, and listen. Use the arrow keys or the buttons to move between steps.

    1 · What

    What is an interval?

    In music, an interval is simply the distance between two notes. We measure space in inches or centimeters; musicians measure sound in intervals. The smallest step on a keyboard, from one key to the very next, is a half step.

    Tap any two keys. The measuring tape counts the half steps between them.

    2 · How: naming

    Two numbers: size and quality

    Every interval name has two parts. The size (2nd, 3rd, 4th…) comes from counting letter names, starting with the bottom note as 1. The quality (major, minor, perfect, augmented, diminished) comes from counting the exact number of half steps. C up to E is a 3rd because it spans three letters, C D E; it is a major 3rd because those letters are 4 half steps apart.

    3 · How: qualities

    Stretching and squeezing an interval

    Change one of the notes by a half step and the size stays the same (the letters don't change) but the quality changes. There are two families:

    • Perfect family (unisons, 4ths, 5ths, octaves): one half step smaller than perfect is diminished; one larger is augmented. There is no major or minor.
    • Major/minor family (2nds, 3rds, 6ths, 7ths): major made a half step smaller is minor; minor made smaller is diminished; major made larger is augmented. Here, minor becomes diminished by raising the bottom note (C♯–E♭), which reads more easily than lowering the top note again (C–E𝄫).

    4 · How: spelling

    Spelling counts

    Two notes that sound the same but are spelled differently are enharmonic: D♯ and E♭ are the same key on the piano. But an interval is named from its spelling, not just its sound. C–D♯ spans two letters (C D), so it's an augmented 2nd; C–E♭ spans three letters (C D E), so it's a minor 3rd. Same 3 half steps, same sound, different interval.

    In scales

    Scales follow the same rule: a major or minor scale uses each letter name once, so each scale degree is the right size above the tonic. In E major the 7th degree is D♯ (a major 7th above E), not E♭ (which would be a diminished 8th and repeat the letter E).

    A shortcut for 5ths (and 4ths)

    On the white keys, every 5th is perfect except one: B up to F, which is a half step smaller (a diminished 5th). So you can spot a perfect 5th from its accidentals alone: if both notes carry the same accidental, the 5th is perfect (both natural, both sharp, both flat, even both double sharp). The only exception is the B–F pair, where the bottom note needs one more sharp (or one less flat) than the top: B♭–F and B–F♯ are perfect.

    4ths work the same way, with the exception reversed: every white-key 4th is perfect except F up to B (augmented), so a 4th whose notes carry the same accidental is perfect unless the letters are F and B. Because a 4th inverts to a 5th, it's really one fact.

    5 · Why: melody

    A melody is a chain of intervals

    At its simplest, a melody is a sequence of intervals: the basic ups and downs of a tune. Watch the ball bounce from note to note; the numbers show how many half steps each hop moves.

    6 · Why: transposition

    Same intervals, new key

    Move a song higher or lower to fit a singer's voice and every note changes, but the intervals stay exactly the same. That's why your brain still recognizes the song instantly.

    7 · How composers use them

    Flip it: interval inversion

    Keep the interval sizes but flip their direction: every “up” becomes a “down,” every “down” an “up.” The result is a fresh variation that still feels connected to the original. Here a motif leaps up a 5th; its inversion leaps down a 5th.

    8 · Why: singing

    Steps are easy, leaps are dramatic

    Interval size matters to singers. Small steps and skips are a smooth, comfortable path for the voice; big leaps are like jumping a canyon: exciting, but much harder to land accurately.

    9 · Why: tension

    Stretch, then resolve

    Big leaps create emotional tension. A classic rule of thumb: after a wide leap upward, a melody feels stretched and wants to come back the other way, usually by step. Here is an original tune that shows the idea: a breathtaking octave leap up, then intervals that get smaller and smaller as the tune cascades down toward the home note.

    10 · Why: harmony

    Intervals all at once: chords

    Intervals also happen at the same time, in chords. A major triad stacks a major 3rd with a minor 3rd above it. Squeeze the middle note down by one half step and the stack flips to minor 3rd + major 3rd: the bright major chord turns dark.

    11 · Why: navigating a key

    How far from home?

    Intervals tell us where we are in a key by measuring the distance from the tonic, the home base. Knowing whether a note is the 3rd, 5th or 7th above home helps musicians find their way through a song. Tap a scale degree to hear it against home.

    12 · Beyond keys

    Intervals as building blocks

    In the 20th century, atonal composers broke free of traditional keys. Instead of scales, they used intervals themselves as the building blocks of new chords and whole compositional systems. On this pitch circle, each shape is built from one kind of interval.
