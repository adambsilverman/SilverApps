How transposition works here
  1. Sounding pitch is the pitch the instrument produces. Written pitch is the note in the part. Concert pitch is where the note name matches the standard pitch (C sounds as C); in practice it means the same as sounding pitch.
  2. Reading a part: move the written note the way the instrument sounds. Clarinet in B♭ sounds a M2 lower, so a written D sounds as C.
  3. Writing a part: go the other way. To get a concert C from a clarinet in B♭, write the D a M2 above.
  4. The key of the instrument is the pitch that sounds when it plays a written C: on a horn in F, a written C sounds as the F below.
  5. Key signatures transpose with the notes. Concert G major becomes A major for B♭ instruments. Horns, trumpets and timpani in older scores often have no key signature at all, but modern scores transpose their signatures like any other instrument.
  6. Octave numbers use middle C = C4. Octave transposers (piccolo, guitar, contrabass…) keep the same note name and key signature and change only the octave.
