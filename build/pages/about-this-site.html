About this site

SilverApps is a set of interactive lessons, quizzes, drills and tools for learning music, made to slot into music courses in high school and college and to work just as well for anyone teaching themselves. A teacher can use one app for a single class or build a whole course around them; a student working alone can start anywhere.

The quizzes respond as students work, explaining each answer, and their scores can go straight to your LMS gradebook (Canvas, D2L Brightspace, Blackboard, Moodle) or reach you as a verified report from the student. Assign a Test Yourself quiz, or build your own test from any of the units with the Test Maker. How to connect to your gradebook.

Every topic works the same way: you try your hand. Instead of reading about an idea and moving on, you hear it, change it and use it right away, in small creative tasks (build a chord, drag a note into place, move an example into another key, put a progression together, run a fragment of your own through a minimalist process) and in close analysis of specific pieces. Classical examples sit alongside pop and rock songs (Taylor Swift, Tracy Chapman, the Supremes, the Beatles, Nirvana), jazz (Gershwin, Fats Waller, Duke Ellington, Wayne Shorter), folk songs and hymns.

The apps also work in a live classroom. Project a lesson and step through it with the class, then turn the drills into a class game: students join on their own phones by scanning a QR code, everyone answers the same question at once, and the room sees how the class did before the answer is revealed. No accounts or app downloads are needed, and answers are anonymous, so a few minutes of drills can open or close any class.

What's on the site

The front page groups the apps by subject: Foundations, Advanced Harmony, Voice Leading, Melody, Phrase and Form, Orchestration, Ear Training and Practice Tools, Creating Music, Music Technology and the Music Business, and Audio Tools. Use the buttons at the top of the front page to jump to a subject, or type in Find a topic. Nothing needs an account or an install; everything runs in the browser, on a laptop, tablet or phone.

Lessons

Most topics start with a lesson: a set of steps you click through in order, with the step list along the top so you can jump back to any of them. Examples play, and most of them can be moved to another key with the key picker, so the same idea can be heard and seen in all twelve. Students can work through a lesson before class, or a teacher can project one and step through it in front of the room. A lesson can be linked to a single step by adding #step= and the step number to its address, which is handy for pointing students at one idea in an assignment.

Test Yourself quizzes

Most lessons have a Test Yourself quiz on its own page, reached from the lesson's last step, from the lesson and drills pages, and from the Test Yourself button on the front page. It's a short open-note quiz: each try draws 12 to 15 questions from a larger bank, with every part of the lesson covered, so a retake isn't the same quiz again. Each question links back to the step that covers it, there's a hint for anyone who wants one, and a miss shows the hint and allows another try. Only first tries count toward the score. Students can use it as a self-check, or a teacher can assign it: students send a Report for my professor from the end screen, or the quiz can be added to a course so its score goes straight into the gradebook (see Gradebook sync below).

Drills

Most topics also have a drills page. Students choose a kind of question and work through as many as they like; each answer is explained, and missed items come back for review. The Your progress panel keeps a running count by question type in the student's own browser and makes a Completion report for my professor with a verification code, so drills can be assigned as practice ("50 questions at 80% or better by Friday") and checked on the Check reports page.

Class games

Most drills pages can also run as a live class game. The teacher chooses Host a class game and shows the room code and QR code on the projector; students join on their phones or laptops, with no sign-in. The teacher sends one question at a time, everyone answers at once, and the answer stays hidden until the teacher reveals it along with the percent of the class that got it right. Answers are anonymous. Students who finish early can work ahead privately on the same drills.

Ear training and practice tools

The ear-training apps cover singing intervals and patterns, chord qualities, melodic dictation and rhythmic dictation, plus performing written rhythms by tapping or clapping. The practice tools (metronome, drone and the Shadow Tuner, which sings a reference pitch back to you) are for daily practice and for rehearsal. The tuner and the clapping drills use the microphone; the browser asks permission first, and nothing is recorded or sent anywhere.

Creating music, technology and the music business

These lessons are about making and releasing music: composition, improvisation, collaboration, lyrics, minimalism, piano accompaniment, music software, pop production, film and game scoring, and distribution and rights. Some come with a hands-on tool, such as the Minimalist Process Lab or the Groove Maker, for building progressions and patterns and hearing them right away. They work well as starting points for projects and class discussion.

Audio tools

The Audio Clip Editor, Video Clip Editor, Multitrack Studio and Vocal Tuner are small editors for preparing class examples and student projects: trimming and normalizing clips, layering recordings over a backing track, tuning a vocal. Files are opened and processed on your own computer and are never uploaded.

For teachers

Everything here is free to use in class and to assign. A typical unit might be: the lesson before class, a class game to check understanding in class, drills for homework with a completion report, and the Test Yourself quiz as a graded check, either through your LMS or through reports. The site can also be copied to your own web space under the license described below.

Who makes it

SilverApps is written and maintained by Adam Silverman (opens in a new tab), a composer who teaches music theory. All of the teaching ideas are his. The site was coded largely with Anthropic's Claude, which also helped organize it and generated much of the text.

The notation

Silverman typeset the music notation on this site. The staves inside the apps are drawn as you work, with VexFlow; the score images in the lessons he engraved himself. The few exceptions, such as a medieval chant manuscript and a page of published sheet music, are credited where they appear.

Most of what you'll see is public-domain music: folk songs, hymns, carols and classical repertoire whose copyrights have expired. The exceptions are short excerpts of copyrighted songs, typeset here only so they can be analyzed. The pop song transcriptions are Silverman's.

The recordings

Some audio was created specifically for a particular lesson; other audio is generated adaptively. The rest are short excerpts of commercial recordings. Videos from YouTube are embedded from their published sources rather than copied here. The piano in generated examples and the sampled guitars, trumpet and trombone come from Nicholas Brosowsky's tonejs-instruments (opens in a new tab) collection, used under CC BY 3.0 (opens in a new tab). The electric pianos in the studio tools are Greg Sullivan's Wurlitzer EP200 samples (CC BY 3.0, mapped by kinwie for the sfzinstruments (opens in a new tab) project) and Jeff Learman's jRhodes3c (opens in a new tab) Rhodes samples (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 (opens in a new tab)). The sampled drums and hand percussion come from the Versilian Community Sample Library (opens in a new tab), which is public domain (CC0). The accordion comes from Frank Wen's FluidR3 General MIDI soundfont, as packaged by the midi-js-soundfonts (opens in a new tab) project (CC BY 3.0 (opens in a new tab)).

Fair use

Short excerpts of copyrighted music, whether a few bars of a score or a few seconds of a recording, are included under fair use, 17 U.S.C. § 107. You'll see a note under each one. All rights remain with the artists and copyright holders.

The claim rests on how the excerpts are used. The site is free and nonprofit, and it exists for teaching. Each excerpt is the smallest passage that shows the point being taught, and it sits next to commentary and analysis of that passage. None of them can stand in for the song, the recording or the published sheet music. If you hold rights to something here and object to its use, contact Silverman through adambsilverman.com (opens in a new tab) and it will be taken down.

Gradebook sync

Most lessons have a Test Yourself quiz. A score can get from that quiz to a course gradebook in two ways. The first is automatic: you add the quiz to your course as a SCORM package, students take it inside the course, and the LMS records the score. The second needs no setup in the LMS: students send you a report, you check the reports and import the scores as a spreadsheet file.

To give a test that covers several units, with no hints and one answer per question, use the Test maker: pick the units and a length, then share the link or download a SCORM package. Its scores reach the gradebook the same two ways.

Quiz scores are first-try scores. A student who misses a question sees the hint and tries again, but only the first try counts. Students can retake a quiz as often as they like; the package and the import file both keep the best score.

Automatic: a SCORM package

Go to Gradebook sync, pick a quiz and download its package. It's a small .zip file. Upload it as it is (don't unzip it). The package doesn't copy the lesson into your course; it opens the live quiz from this site, so students need an internet connection, and corrections to a lesson reach them without a new upload. The score arrives as a percent. The "Look back" links open the lesson in a new tab, since the quiz is open-note.

D2L Brightspace
  1. In Content, open the module where the quiz belongs and choose Add Existing (or Upload / Create in older courses), then SCORM/xAPI Object.
  2. Choose Bulk Upload, upload the .zip, select it and choose Next.
  3. Answer Yes to creating a grade item. Under the calculation method, choose the highest attempt. Choose Add.
  4. Brightspace makes a new grade item outside any category. In Grades > Manage Grades, edit it to move it into a category and set its points. Or, from the topic's menu in Content, choose Edit or Link to Existing to send the score to a grade item you already have.
Canvas

Canvas plays SCORM packages through its SCORM tool, which your school has to have turned on.

  1. In the course, go to Settings > Navigation, drag SCORM into the visible list and save.
  2. Open SCORM, choose Upload and pick the .zip.
  3. From Choose import type, choose Import as graded assignment, then Go. This can't be changed later.
  4. The new assignment is worth 100 points and is unpublished. In Assignments, edit it to set the points, group and due date, then publish it.
Blackboard
  1. On Course Content, select the plus sign, then Create > SCORM package.
  2. Choose Upload SCORM package and pick the .zip.
  3. In the SCORM settings, turn on Grade SCORM, choose the grading schema and set the maximum score to 100. Set attempts to unlimited and grade on the highest attempt. The grade settings can't be changed after the package is added, so set them now.

In Blackboard's Original Course View, open a content area and choose Build Content > Content Package (SCORM). Turn grading on in the package settings.

Moodle
  1. Turn editing on and choose Add an activity or resource > SCORM package.
  2. Upload the .zip under Package file.
  3. Under Grade, set the grading method to Highest grade and the maximum grade to 100. Under Attempts management, allow unlimited attempts and grade on the highest attempt.

Whatever the LMS, try the quiz once as a student (Student View in Canvas and Blackboard, View as Student in Brightspace) and check that the score shows up before you assign it. Some schools block SCORM uploads or limit them to certain roles; if the upload option is missing, ask your LMS support office.

By hand: reports and an import file

At the end of any quiz, a student can press Report for my professor. The report has the lesson, the student's name, their latest and best first-try scores, the questions they missed and a verification code. They can email it, download it, print it or copy it into an assignment box. Practice apps make a similar completion report from the Your progress panel.

Ask students to type the ID your LMS uses to identify them in the report's Student ID or campus username box. In Brightspace that's the Username or the OrgDefinedId; in Canvas, the SIS Login ID or SIS User ID; in Blackboard, the username.

  1. Open Check reports. Paste in all the reports, one after another, or choose the report files students downloaded. Each one is marked "Checks out" or "Doesn't match".
  2. Under Gradebook import file, choose your LMS and whether to grade as a percent or as the number of questions right. Each lesson becomes one grade column. To fill a grade item you already have, type its name exactly as it appears in your gradebook.
  3. Download the .csv file and import it: in Brightspace, Grades > Enter Grades > Import; in Canvas, Grades > Import; in Blackboard Ultra, the gradebook's Upload button; in Moodle, Grades > Import > CSV file, matching the "Student ID or username" column to the username or ID number field.

Brightspace and Blackboard match students only by ID, so reports without one are left out of those files and listed on the page for you to enter by hand. Canvas and Moodle files keep them, with the name, but matching by name is less reliable. A Brightspace grade item graded as points should be worth as many points as the quiz has questions; graded as a percent, 100.

Privacy

The site counts visits with Umami: which pages are opened, and the city, region and country they're opened from. No names, no email addresses, no cookies. Your practice settings, progress and quiz scores stay in your own browser unless you send a report or take a quiz from inside your course's LMS, which then receives the score.
