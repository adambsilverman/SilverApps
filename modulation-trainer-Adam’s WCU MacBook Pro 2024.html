Field guide: modulation methods
How to decide: a process of elimination

    When more than one method seems to apply, choose the one most evident in the music, and favor the more typical paradigm: common-chord modulations are routine; common-tone modulations are rare.

    Labeling modulations
    1. Pivot modulation. Label the pivot chord with two stacked Roman numerals: the top shows its function in the old key, the bottom its function in the new key. Write the new key's name at the pivot; all later numerals belong to the new key.
    2. Enharmonic pivot. Stack both chord spellings above the staff, including the one that isn't notated, and stack the numerals below. A respelled dim7 changes its root, so its inversion figures can change too (B in the bass is the root of B°7 but the third of G♯°7).
    3. Direct modulation. Write the new key's name under the beat where the new key's harmony begins. Turnaround chords that set up the new key belong to the new key; they aren't pivots.
    4. Minor keys. Numerals in minor follow natural minor: III, VI and VII are the major chords a minor 3rd, minor 6th and minor 7th above the tonic, with no flat (in A minor, C major is III). The raised leading tone gives V and vii°. A flat appears only for chords borrowed into a major key (♭III, ♭VI).
    5. Tonicization. No new key name: use secondary numerals such as V65/V in the home key. A tonicized V before a half cadence isn't a modulation.
