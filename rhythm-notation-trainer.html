How rhythm is notated here
  1. Show the beat. Note values and beams should make every beat visible, so a player can find it at sight. A note that starts between beats and is held through the next beat is split at the beat and tied.
  2. Simple meters (2/4, 3/4, 4/4) have quarter-note beats that divide in two. Beam each beat on its own. Plain eighths may share a beam across beats 1–2 or 3–4 of 4/4, or across a whole bar of 2/4 or 3/4, but never across the middle of 4/4.
  3. Compound meters (6/8, 9/8, 12/8) have dotted-quarter beats that divide in three. Beam the three eighths of each beat together. No note may cross a beat unless it fills whole beats, like a dotted half in 6/8.
  4. A long note that starts on the beat may be written as one value: a half note on beat 1 or 3 of 4/4, a dotted quarter on beat 1 (or beat 3 of 4/4), a dotted or double-dotted half on beat 1. In 4/4, show the middle of the bar; the one exception is the familiar quarter–half–quarter.
  5. Dotted notes belong on strong beats. A dotted note on a weak beat hides the next strong beat; write it as a tied pair instead.
  6. Rests show each beat too. Finish the current beat with small rests, then use one rest per beat (a half rest may cover beats 1–2 or 3–4 of 4/4, and a whole rest fills any empty bar).
  7. Hemiola deliberately groups the beats against the meter, so you will see three half notes across two bars of 3/4, or three quarters in a bar of 6/8.

Terms

Anacrusis
A pickup: one or more notes before the first downbeat of a phrase. The last bar is often shortened to balance it.
Syncopation
A note that begins on an unaccented part of the bar or beat and is held into a more strongly accented one.
Hemiola
A rhythm that shifts the feel of the meter, usually three notes in the place of two or two in the place of three.
Simple / compound
Beats that divide in two / in three. In 6/8 the eighth is the division; the dotted quarter is the beat.
Duple / triple / quadruple
Two, three or four beats in a bar.
